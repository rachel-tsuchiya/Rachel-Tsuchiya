# FX Hedging Executive Brief: Scenario 27 — LNG Marketer

**To:** Chief Financial Officer  
**From:** Financial Analyst  
**Date:** October 23, 2026  
**Subject:** Managing Foreign Exchange Risk on EUR 21.5 Million Receivable

## Exposure and Business Risk

Our U.S.-based liquefied natural gas (LNG) company has invoiced a German utility for EUR 21,500,000, with payment expected in 91 days. Since our company operates in U.S. dollars but will receive payment in euros, we face foreign exchange risk. Although the euro payment is fixed, its value in U.S. dollars will fluctuate depending on the EUR/USD exchange rate at settlement.

If the euro depreciates against the U.S. dollar, our company will receive fewer dollars, potentially reducing revenue and profitability. For example, if the exchange rate decreases from 1.15 to 1.10 USD/EUR, the value of our receivable would decline from $24,725,000 to $23,650,000, representing a $1,075,000 loss in expected dollar proceeds. This demonstrates why managing currency exposure is important for maintaining financial stability.

## Hedging Alternatives

Three strategies could help protect our company against unfavorable exchange-rate movements.

**1. Forward Contract:** We could enter a 91-day forward contract to sell EUR 21.5 million at the indicative rate of 1.1653 USD/EUR. This would secure approximately $25,053,950 in proceeds, providing certainty for budgeting and financial planning. However, we would sacrifice potential gains if the euro appreciates before settlement.

**2. Money-Market Hedge:** Our company could borrow the present value of the euro receivable today, convert the borrowed euros into U.S. dollars at the current spot rate, and invest those dollars for 91 days. This strategy can lock in future dollar proceeds and provide an alternative to a forward contract. However, it requires borrowing capacity, additional transactions, and consideration of both EUR and USD interest rates.

**3. Currency Put Option:** We could purchase a put option giving us the right, but not the obligation, to sell euros at a predetermined strike price. This would protect against euro depreciation while allowing us to benefit from appreciation. However, the option requires an upfront premium of $0.0147 per euro, totaling $316,050, which reduces our net proceeds.

## Assumptions and Hypothesis

For this preliminary analysis, I assume the receivable will be paid in full after 91 days, the indicative forward rate is available, and the stated option premium remains unchanged. Current spot exchange rates and applicable USD and EUR interest rates still need to be researched and verified.

I expect the forward hedge to provide the most predictable dollar proceeds, while the put option will preserve potential upside at a higher cost. I will test this hypothesis by comparing net USD proceeds across different exchange-rate scenarios. If the modeled results show that another strategy provides greater certainty or lower effective costs under the stated assumptions, my hypothesis would need to be revised.
